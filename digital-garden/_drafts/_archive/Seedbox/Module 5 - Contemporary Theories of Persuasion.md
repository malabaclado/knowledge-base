# Module 5 - Contemporary Theories of Persuasion

==The transition from classical to contemporary rhetoric involves the a shift of focus from the source/rhetor to the audience.== It is an exploration of new ways of understanding the human condition, as the dominant scientific dogmas and systems at that time, failed to encapsulate social values and formations from a humanist point of view (Hauser, 2002; Herrick, 2000).

> “Knowing your audience means learning what’s on their minds, what their concerns are and what’s important to them. You need to know who they are and be able to speak their language, at least to some degree. In fact, creating a presentation often includes an element of “translation” from your frame of reference into the language and context of your audience.” (Sierra Leadership, n.d.)

## Bitzer's Rhetorical Situation
Bitzer (1968) defines rhetorical situation as
> “a complex of persons, events, objects, and relations presenting an actual or potential *exigence* which can be completely or partially removed if discourse, introduced into the situation, can so constrain human decision or action as to bring about the significant modification of the exigence” (p. 6).

He emphasizes that *rhetorical situations give rise to discourse*.

### Three elements constitute the rhetorical situation

**Exigence** - imperfection, obstacle marked by urgency; requires discourse to be positively modified

**Audience** - people who are intended to be influenced by the discourse; have the capacity to be mediators of change

**Constraints** - “persons, events, objects, and relations which … have the power to constrain decision and action needed to modify the exigence” (p. 8)

## Social Judgment Theory

Social Judgment Theory explains *when* persuasive messages are most likely to succeed and *how* people make judgments about them.

In this theory, a person's decision falls on a continuum which is categorized into three latitudes: acceptance, rejection and non-commitment (meanings are exactly as they are named).

A person's initial inclination is called the **anchor position**. The goal of the speaker is to move this anchor position to his liking. One decisive factor in moving this anchor is what we call **ego involvement**. This is how important the issue is to you, how invested you are. The higher your ego involvement, the less likely you are to move away from your anchor position.

## Elaboration Likelihood Model

When we listen to a speaker, we look at three characteristics:
- Message charateristics - how good is the message?
- Source characteristics - how good is the speaker?
- Target characteristics - how interested are we as listener? This one pertains to ourself as audience.

Elaboration Likelihood Model explains how attitudes are formed and how they can be changed.

According to this model, all information goes to two different routes: the central route and the peripheral route. If the audience (or target characteristics) is motivated, it goes to cental route. Otherwise it goes to peripheral route. 

![[Pasted image 20220325002946.png]]

It is theorized that information passing through the central route have higher chance of creating lasting change in attitude while information going through the peripheral route may or may not trigger a change, and if it did, it is only temporary.
