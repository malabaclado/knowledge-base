- One key secret that every great TED talk speaker has: **Your task to the speaker is to instill to your audience an idea** 
- **What is an idea?** Think of it as a pattern of information that helps you understand the world.
- IF ideas are communicated properly, **they're capable of changing forever how someone things about the world**. 
- Ideas are the most powerful force shaping human culture.
- **4 simple guides to communicating an idea:**
		1. Limit your talk to 1 major idea.
		2. Give your listeners a reason to care. Instill curiosity. Use provocative questions. If you reveal a disconnection in someone's worldview, they'll feel the need to bridge that knowledge gap. 
		3. Build your idea piece by piece, using fundamental concepts. Metaphors play a central role for this. 
		4. Make your idea worth sharing. Who does this idea benefit?

**Step 5: Identify your main points**

![[Pasted image 20220625183025.png]]

A common mistake in preparing a speech outline is to start with the introduction. The more efficient way, however, is to identify your main points first. This way, you will have a more definite picture of what you’re supposed to introduce.

For your 2-3 minute speech, two main points will do. To decide on what your main points should be, start with your claim. 

For example, if your claim is to add an additional layer in freshmen admissions in your specific degree program, the natural question in your audience’s minds would be, **WHY**? (Note: While writing this module, the UPD administration is reviewing the program-specific admission requirements of applicants, on top of their HS grades). 

List down the possible answers to the WHY question then choose two that you think are most essential in relation to your speech goal. Other questions to consider may be — HOW or WHAT, which address  alternatives, solutions, and evidence.

The following are examples of **organizational patterns** that are considered more appropriate for persuasive speeches:

**Problem-solution** — for speeches wherein you aim “to convince the audience that a problem exists and that your solution would solve or lessen the problem” (DeVito, 2015, p. 324). In this pattern, the first main point would be the problem, the second would be the solution. It provides answers to both WHY and HOW (proposed solutions to the problem).

**Cause-effect/Effect-cause** — for speeches “to convince your audience of the causal connection existing between two events or elements” (DeVito, 2015, p. 324). In this pattern, one main point would be the cause, the other would be the effect.

**Claim-proof** — for speeches which aim to “prove the truth or usefulness of a particular proposition” (DeVito, 2015, p. 327).  This pattern is frequently seen in trials. Among Aristotle’s branches of rhetoric, this may be categorized under  judicial or forensic.

**Monroe’s Motivated Sequence** — a modified, five-step problem-solution format which employs emotional (pathos) and logical (logos) appeals. The sequence of motivational appeals are: (1) attention — gaining audience’s attention; (2) need — exigence, problem; (3) satisfaction — proposed solution; (4) visualization — what will happen if solution is applied/not applied; and (5) action — specific behavior or course of action the audience should take. To read further, click **[HERE](https://uvle.upd.edu.ph/pluginfile.php/463347/mod_label/intro/Jaffe%20%282016%29_Monroe%20motivated%20sequence_pp%20246-247.pdf)**.

**Step 6: Identify your sub points based on your supporting materials**

Since you will be delivering a short speech, go directly to the supporting evidence needed under each main point. Review the supporting materials that you have gathered so far (as you indicated in Module 6). Are these sufficient to substantiate your main points? If not, you may have to do further research. 

Remember that the main points and supporting evidence should be in sentence form. To add credence to your evidence, cite the source of the information. 

Examples: 

A recent study by the UP OCTA research team suggests… 

In a Philippine Star interview, former UP Diliman Chancellor Michael Tan stated …

In a personal conversation I had with a person who recovered from COVID-19… 

As a working student, I experienced …

**Step 7: Formulate your introduction and conclusion**

A well-crafted introduction serves the following purposes:

-   Gain the attention of your audience and motivate them to listen  
    
-   Establish rapport or connection with your audience  
    
-   Establish your ethos as a speaker, your knowledge, experience, interest on the issue  
    
-   State your claim and preview your main points  
    

Your introduction should be brief (less than 20 seconds) so as not to take away time that should be allotted for the meat of your speech — your main ideas and supporting evidence.

If your introduction serves as a preview of your main ideas, your conclusion serves as closure. Make sure that you do not provide new information in your conclusion. Simply summarize your main points and reiterate your claim.
