# Week 1 Data Management Principles
## Data Management Principles
> [!NOTE]
> **Data Management** is the development, execution, and supervision of plans, policies, programs, and practices that deliver, control, protect, and enhance the value of data and information assets throughout their life cycles. *(As defined by DAMA International)*

> [!NOTE]
> A **Data Management Professional** is any person who works in any facet of data management.

Examples: *Database administrators, Network administrators, Programmers, Data Stewards, Data Strategists, Chief Data Officers.*

## Objectives of Data Management
- **Understanding and supporting the information needs** of enterprise and its stakeholders.
- **Capturing, storing, protecting and ensuring integrity** of data assets.
- **Ensuring the quality of data** and information.
- **Ensuring the privacy and confidentiality** of stakeholder data
- **Preventing unauthorized or inappropriate access**, manipulation, or use of data and information.
- **Ensuring data can be used effectively** to add value to the enterprise.

## Data, Metadata and Data Representation

Data
- **defined as** *information stored in digital form*.
- is a *means of representation*
- the raw material in information

> *"Data both is an interpretation of the objects it represents and an object that must be interpreted."*

This means data needs **context**.

Metadata - *"data about the data"*

**There are multiple representations of data** - hence the need for data architecture, stewards and management principles - *to bring consistency to data*.

### Data vs Information
- Data - "the raw material of information"
- Information - *"data in context"*
- In reality, these two concepts intertwine:
	- "Data is a form of information, and information is a form of data"

### Data as an asset
- An asset is an economic resource that holds economic value
- Businesses use data to:
	- Understand their customers
	- Create new products and services
	- Improve operational efficiency
- Data is now an "asset" because of the value it brings to organizations - more and more organizations are now using data to drive their decision making.

## Data Management Principles

### Salient features of data management
1. Data is an asset with **unique properties**
	- *It is not consumed when it is used.*
2. The value of data can and should be expressed in **economic** terms
	- If data is an asset => then data has value => ==organizations must learn how to quantify high quality data==
3. Managing data means managing the **quality** of data.
4. It takes **metadata** to manage data.
5. It takes **planning** to manage data.
6. Data management is **cross-functional**.
7. Data management must **comprise enterprise perspective**.
8. Data management must **account for a range of perspectives**.
9. Data management is a **lifecycle management**.
	- Data has a life cycle. Data processes needs to account for the data lifecycle.
10. Different types of data have **different life cycle characteristics**.
	- Data management must recognize this various characteristics and be flexible enough to accomodate them
11. Managing data includes **managing risks** associated with data.
	- Data could be lost, stolen or misused. Organizations must consider the ethical implications of their data.
	- Data-related risks must be managed as part of the data lifecycle.
12. Data management requirements must **drive information technology decisions**.
	- Managing data requires an approach that technology "serves" rather than drive the organization's data needs. ==This just means that tech should be used for the organization's needs not the other way around.==
13. Effective data management requires leadership commitment.

## Data Management Challenges

### Data differs from other assets
1. Data is not tangible, *yet it is durable.*
2. Data is easy to copy and transport. *Because it is very easy to be reproduce, it can even be stolen without being gone.*
3. Data is dynamic and can be used for multiple purposes. *it may even be used by multiple people at the same time, something that is impossible with physical and/or financial assets.*

### Data risk
- Data *may be inaccurate*
- Data *may be misunderstood*
- Information gaps - which is the difference between what we know and what we need to know to make an effective decision.

## Data management strategy
 

> [!NOTE]
>  What is a strategy?
> 	- A strategy is a **set of choices and decisions** that together chart a high-level course of action to achieve high-level goals.

A **data strategy should include:** business plans to use information to competetive advantage and support enterprise goals.

### Data strategy should come from:
1. What data does the organization needs?
2. How will it get the data?
3. How will it manage to ensure its reliability over time?
4. How will it utilize it?

### Components of a data management startegy should include:
1. A **compelling vision** for data management.
2. A **summary business case** for data management, with selected examples.
3. **Guiding principles**, values and management perspectives.
4. **Mission** and **long-term directional goals of data management**.
5. Proposed **measures of data management success**
6. A SMART short-term (12-24 months) **data management program objectives**
7. **Descriptions of data management roles and organizations**, along with a summary of their responsibilities and decision rights.
8. **Descriptions of data management program components** and initiatives.
9. A **prioritized program of work** with scope boundaries
10. A **draft implementation roadmap** with projects and action items.

### Strategic Planning Deliverables for Data Management 
- **Data management charter** - Overall vision, business case, goals, etc
- **Data management scope statement** - Goals and objectives for some planning horizon (usually 3 years) and the roles, organization, and individual leaders accountable for achieveing these objectives.
- **Data management implementation roadmap** - identifying specific programs, projects, tasks and delivery milestones.

> [!NOTE]- Learning objectives

- Ethics are principles of behavior based on ideas of right and wrong. 
- Ethical principles focus on ideas such as:
	- Fairness
	- Respect
	- Responsibility
	- Integrity
	- Quality
	- Reliability
	- Transparency
	- Trust
- Unethical data handling may result in loss of customers.
- Data ethics are *a matter of social responsibility*.
- ## Core concepts in data handling
	- Impact on people
		- Data represents people; Makes decisions that affects people's lives.
	- Potential for misuse
	- Economic value of data
- ## Two laws that impact data handling
	- [[Data Privacy Act of the Philippines]]
	- [[Anti-Cyber Crime Law of 2012]]
- There is an **ethical imperative** not to only protect data, but also manage its quality.
- Data management professionals have an ethical responsibility to manage data in a way that reduces the risk that it may be misrepresented, be misused, or be misunderstood.
- **Data handling ethics as business drivers.** Ethical data handling can increase the trustworthiness of an organization and the organization's data and process outcomes. 
	- This can create better relationships between the organization and its stakeholders.
- Handling data ethically requires **organization-wide recognition of the risks** associated with misuse of data and **organization commitment to handling data based on principles** that protect individuals and respect the imperatives related to data ownership
- ## Ethical data concepts for online interactions
	- **Ownership of data** - The rights to control one's personal data 
	- **The right to be forgotten** - ability to delete data from the web
	- **Identity** - Having the right to expect one identity, a correct identity and to opt for a private identity.
	- **Freedom of speech online**
- ## Unethical Data Practices
	- **Timing** (Market Timing)
		- Lie through omission of inclusion of certain data points based on timing.
		- Example: Stock market can be manipulated to artificially raise the stock price at the end of the stock trade.
	- **Misleading visualizations**
		- Changing scales
		- Leaving data points out
		- Comparing two things without clarifying relationship
	- **Bias**
		- Using data without addressing the ways in which the bias could be introduced can compuound prejudice while reducing transparency in the process. Giving the resulting outcomes the veneer of impartiality or neutrality, when infact, they are not neutral.
		- ## Types of bias
			- Data collection for pre-defined result
			- Biased use of data collected 
				- Some data discarded if it does not comform with the approach
			- Hunch ans search
				- The analyst has a hunch and wants to satisfy the hunch **but uses only data that confirms the hunch and does not account for other possibilities**.
			- Biased sampling methodology
				- Bias can be intoduced in the ways of selecting the sample set.
				- it is virtually impossible for humans to sample without bias of some sort. To limit bias, use statistical tools to select samples and establish adequate sample sizes. 
			- Context and culture
	- **Obfuscation/Redaction of data**
		- It is the practice of making information anonymous, or removing sensitive information.
		- This risk is present in:
			- Data aggregation
			- Data marking
			- Data masking
- ## Establishing an ethical data culture requires
	- 1. Understanding **existing practices**
	- 2. Defining **expected behaviors**
	- 3. Codifying these **policies and a code of ethics**
	- 4. Providing **training and oversight** to enforce expected behaviors
- ## Steps in establishing an ethical data culture
	- 1. **Review current practices**
		- Purpose: To understanda the *degree* to which they are directly connected to ethical and compliance drivers.
		- This review should:
			- identify how well employyes understand the ethical implications of existing practices in building and preserving the trust of customers, partners, and other stakeholders.
			- document ethical principles that underlie the organization's collection, use and oversight of data.
	- 2. **Identify principles, practices and risk factors.**
		- Practices should be supported by controls, such as:
			- Guiding principles
			- Risk
			- Practice
			- Control
	- 3. **Create a data ethics strategy and roadmap**
		- ## Component pieces of a data ethics strategy
			- 1. **Values statements** - *describes what the organization believes in*
			- 2. **Ethical data handling principles** - *describes how to approach challenges presented by data*
			- 3. **Compliance framework** - *factors that drive organizational obligations*
			- 4. **Risk assessments** - *the likelihood and the implications of specific problems arising within the organization*
			- 5. **Training and communications** - training must be ongoing and communications should reach all employees
			- 6. **Roadmap** - a timeline with activities approved by management
				- Activities include:
					- 1. Execution of the training and communications plan
					- 2. Identification and remediation of gaps in existing practices
					- 3. Risk mitigation
					- 4. Monitoring plans
			- 7. **Approach to auditing and monitoring**
				- It is advisable to monitor specific activities **to ensure they are being executed** in compliance with ethical principles.

- **Data governance** is defined as: the exercise of authority and control (as in planning, monitoring and enforcement) over the management of data assets.
- **The purpose of data governance** is to ensure that data is managed properly, according to policies and best practices.
- **The role of data governance in data management.** The function of data management is to ensure an organization gets value out of its data. Data governance focuses on how decisions are made about data and how people and processes are expected to behave in relation to data.  
- ## Principles of Data Governance
	- Strategy
	- Policy
	- Standards and quality
	- Oversight (aka stewardship)
	- Compliance
	- Issue management
	- Data management projects
	- Data asset valuation
- Data governance most often focus on reducing risks and improving processes.
	- ## Reducing Risk
		- **General risk management.** Oversight of the risks data poses to finances or reputation, including response to legal and regulatory issues.
		- **Data security.** Protection of data assets through controls for the availability, usability, integrity, consistency, auditability and security of data.
		- **Privacy.** Control of private/confidential/personal identifying information through policy and compliance monitoring.
	- ## Improving Processes
		- Regulatory compliance
		- Data quality improvement
		- Metadata management
		- Efficiency in development projects
		- Vendor management

# Week 5 Data Architecture
- Architecture
	- refers to the art and science of building things (esp habitable structures) and to the results of the process of building - the bulidings themselves.
	- in a more general sense, it refers to an organized arrangement of component elements intended to optimize the function, performance, feasibility, cost and aesthetics of an overall structure or system.
	- defined as the fundamental organization of a system, embodied in its components, their relationships to each other and the environment, and the principles governing its design and evolution.
- The term architecture has been adoted to describe several facets of information systems design.
- An organization's data architecture is described by an integrated collection of master-design documents at different levels of abstraction.
- The **goal of data architecture** is to be the bridge between business startegy and technology execution. 
- Data architects:
	- Strategically prepare organizations to quickly evolve their products, services, and data to take advantage of business opportunities inherant in emerging technologies.
	- Translate business needs into data and system requirements so that processses consistently have the data they require.
	- Manage complex data and information delivery throughout the enterprise.
	- facilitate alignment between business and IT.
	- Acts as agents for change, transformation and agility.
- ## Primary data architecture outcomes
	- Data storage and processing requirements
	- Designs of structures and plans that meet the current and long-term data requirements of the enterprise.
- To reach the above outcomes, data architects define and maintain specifications that:
	- 1. Define the current state of data in the organization.
	- 2. Provide a standard business vocabulary for data and components.
	- 3. Align data architecture with enterprise strategy and business architecture.
	- 4. Express strategic data requirements.
	- 5. Outline high-level integrated designs to meet the requirements.
	- 6. Integrate with overall enterprise architecture roadmap.
- **Architects design in a way that generates value to the organization.** This value may come from:
	- optimal technical footprint
	- operational and project efficiencies
	- increased ability of the organization to use its data
- **An overall data architecture practice includes**
	- Using data architecture artifacts to define data requirements, guide data integration, control data assets, and align data investments with business strategy.
	- Collaborating with, learning from and influencing various stakeholders that are engaged with improving the business or IT systems environment.
	- Using data architecture to establish the semantics of an enterprise, via a common business vocabulary.
- The Enterprise Data Architecture must include both Enterprise Data Models, as well as Data Flow Design.
	- 1. Enterprise Data Model (EDM)
		- Includes key enterprise data entities.
	- 2. Data Flow Design
		- Defines the requirements and master blueprint for storage and processing across databases, applications, platforms and networks.
- ## Data Flow Design
	- **Data flows** are a type of data lineage documentation that depicts how data moves through business processes and systems.
	- **End-to-end data flows** illustrate where the data originated, where it is stored and used, and how it is transformed as it moves inside and between diverse processes and systems.
	- Data flows can be documented at different levels of detail:
		- Subject area
		- Business entity
		- Attibute level
	- Systems can be represented by 
		- network segments
		- platforms
		- common application sets
		- individual servers
- ## Data architecture tools
	- Data modelling tools
		- Includes lineage and relation tracking functions
		- Enable architects to manage linkages between models created for different pusposes and at different levels of abstraction
	- Asset Management Software
		- Used to inventory items, describe their content, and track relationships between them.
		- Enables an organization to ensure that it follows contractual obligations related to software licenses.
		- These tools collect and contain valuable metadata about systems and the data they contain.
	- Graphical design applications
		- Used to create architecturela design diagrams, data flows, data value chains and other architectural artifacts.
- ## Techniques in Data Architecture
	- Lifecycle projections
	- Diagramming clarity
- ## Implementing data architecture
	- Organizing the enterprise data architecture teams and forums
	- Producing the initial versions of data architecture artifacts such as enterprise data model, enterprise-wide data flow and roadmaps.
	- Forming and establishing a data architectural way of working in development projects.
	- Creating awareness throughout the organization of the value of data architecture efforts.
- ## Readiness assessment/ Risk assessment
	-  The most significant risks are:
		- 1. Lack of management support
		- 2. No proven record of accomplishment
		- 3. Apprehensive sponsor
		- 4. Counter-productive executive decisions
		- 5. Culture shock
		- 6. Inexperienced project leader
		- 7. Dominance of a one-dimensional view
- The speed with which an organization adopts architectural practices depends on how adaptive its culture is.
- **Organization and cultural change.** The abulity of an organization to adopt Data Architecture practices depends on several factors:
	- 1. Cultural **receptivity to architectural approach** *(developing an architecture-friendly approach)*
	- 2. Organizational **recognition of data as a business asset**, not just an IT concern.
	- 3. Organizational **ability to let go of a local perspective and adopt an enterprise perspective on data**.
	- 4. Organizational a**bility to integrate architectural deliverables into project methodology**
	- 5. **Level of acceptance of formal data governance**
	- 6. **Ability to look holistically at the enterprise**, rather than being focused solely on project delivery and IT solutioning.
- Data architecture activities directly support the alignment and control of data.
- Data architects often as *business liasons* for data governance activities.
- Enterprise data architecture and data governance activities of the organzation have to be well-aligned.
- **Data architecture governance** activities includes:
	- **Overseeing projects** - *ensure projects comply*
	- **Managing architectural designs, lifecycle and tools**
	- **Defining standards** - set rules for how data is used within the organization.
	- **Creating data-related artifacts** - *artifacts that enable compliance with governance directives*
- Performance **metrics on enterprise data architecture** reflect architectural goals.
- ## Metrics
	- Architecture standard compliance rate - measures how closely projects comply  with estabnlished data architectures.
	- Implementation trends - track the degree to which enterprise architecture has imprved the organziation's ability to implement projects.
	- Business value measurements - track progress toward expected business effects and benefits.
