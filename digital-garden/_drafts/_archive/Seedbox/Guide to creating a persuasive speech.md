# Guide to creating a persuasive speech

This guide follows the principle of the [[Five canons of rhetoric]], namely: Invention, Disposition, Style, Memory and Delivery. 

Considering the audience is central to all speechmaking, especially persuasive speaking.

### Step 1

**Step 1 Notes: Deciding on your persuasive speech topic**

After reflecting on the guide questions in selecting a topic (Beebe & Beebe, 2016), provide your responses to the following:

1.  What’s your final topic? - For now, a phrase will do
2.  Why are you stirred by this issue? - Your personal experience
3.  Why is it important to engage in discourse regarding this issue? - Going beyond your personal experience, this focuses on exigence or the NEED for discourse
4.  What is your personal stand on this issue? - This would help you format your speech claim or proposition later 
5.  What do you think are your audience’s thoughts on this issue? - Audience analysis to help you decide on persuasive strategies

### Step 2

**Step 2: Formulate your persuasive speech claim or proposition**

Jaffe (2016) defines claim as “a debatable point or proposal, conclusion, or generalization that some people won’t accept without some sort of evidence or backing” (p. 236). 

In research papers, your claim or proposition is also referred to as your argument. I’m sure that you have come across journal articles where the author states “I argue that…” or “It is my contention that…” after giving a brief backgrounder on the topic. 

In persuasive speeches, you will be doing the same. Your claim, proposition, or argument should be clearly stated at the end of your introduction, before moving on to your main points. This way, the direction you want to take will be clear in the minds of your audience. 

There are three types of claims or propositions: fact, value, and policy. See the table below (Beebe & Beebe, 2016, p. 364) for the examples. Note that these are similar to the principles of classical rhetoric discussed in Module 4.

**Step 2 Notes: Formulating your claim or proposition**

Based on your final speech topic, formulate your claim or proposition in one simple sentence. Similar to the examples in the table, the statement should contain a **single idea**.

### Step 3 
**Step 3: Formulate your persuasive goal**

Beebe & Beebe (2016) define persuasion as **“the process of changing or reinforcing attitudes, beliefs, values, or behavior”** (p. 345). This highlights the importance of knowing where the members of your audience stand on the issue you will discuss, since it will dictate the direction of your speech — to change or to reinforce. It is important to remember that **your claim or proposition and your goal complement each other.** 

1.  Attitudes — learned predisposition to respond favorably or unfavorably toward something (p. 345)
2.  Beliefs — something you understand to be true or false (p. 346)
3.  Values — an enduring concept of right or wrong, good or bad (p. 346)
4.  Behaviors — change in or reinforcement of attitude, belief, and value, does not always translate to changes in behavior. While some may be moved to believe your claim, acting on it would require a careful study of their behavioral psychology. For this reason, change in behavior is quite difficult.

**Step 3 Notes: Formulating your persuasive goal**

Review your responses in Steps 1 & 2 Notes. Based on these, formulate your persuasive speech goal.

Examples: 

To reinforce the supportive attitude of my classmates towards contributing to community pantries.

To change my classmates’ belief that social media platforms have become toxic spaces. (This may be based on your classmates’ responses in the Module 6 forum)
