## Core Concepts of Rhetoric

Rhetoric started in the Greek colony of Sicily around 466 b.c.e. 


The Sophists taught their students **“how to make the weaker argument stronger”**, banking on the humanist purpose of rhetoric where the aim of communication is to benefit the speaker and the audience at the present. Because of this, Sophists are criticized for focusing on **style and presentation at the expense of truth**. 

 **Plato’s rhetoric is idealist.** He wanted to ban all rhetoric that was not based on a prior philosophical inquiry into the ideal, the beautiful, the true, and the genuine. For him, rhetoric was a tool to educate, and not to please, an audience. 

**Aristotle’s rhetoric was realist** in nature. During his time, Sophistical optimism and Platonian skepticism already came to an end. Aristotle’s Athens was a democracy, filled with intellectuals trying to make sense of the tragic history of their nation. The goal of rhetoric was to bring harmony and determine the best judgment in every situation. Aristotle then defined rhetoric as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion.

## Aristotle’s branches of rhetoric

-  In **deliberative or legislative rhetoric**, the speaker attempts to get an audience to take or not to take an action.
	- In short, the topic at hand is future-oriented. Debating about issues and providing which action steps to take focus on the things to come and what the audience can do to influence the outcome. An example is the senate hearing on red-tagging. 
-  **Judicial or forensic rhetoric** is concerned with determining whether an accusation or charge is just or unjust.
	-  Its focus is to determine whether an action that happened in the past is good or bad, and how we can deal with this at the present. According to Nordquist (2009), judicial rhetoric is usually employed by lawyers and judges to determine the core value of justice. Judicial rhetoric can also be seen in everyday life messages, such as the `#JunkTerrorLaw` social media posts. Through this hashtag, people tried to prove that the passage of the law was unconstitutional. Oral arguments were likewise made at the Supreme Court to contend that the law should be void for vagueness.
-  **Epideictic or ceremonial rhetoric** deals with praising or blaming persons or entities in order to celebrate, mourn, or to purge guilt.
	- It largely concerns itself with speeches and writings such as obituaries, leaders’ statements during crises, letters of recommendation and sometimes even literary works (Nordquist, 2009). An example of speeches to address crisis would be President Duterte's televised speeches on COVID-19 vaccines. More often than not, he received negative feedback because the public perceived his language and actions as lacking, often putting blame on the wrong government officials.

Rhetoric critics observed that **specific situations usually call for the production of a specific type of rhetoric.** Audiences expect to hear messages from rhetors (speakers) that are fitting to the situation.

## Aristotle’s modes of persuasion 

**Ethos**
How you convince your audience with credibility. This is your personal branding.
Build credibility through testimonies.

**Demostrate ethos through:**
Intelligence - has authority on field
Integrity - trusted personality
Goodwill - has audiences' best interest at heart

**Pathos**
Connect to viewers by evoking an emotional response

*How can I get the audience emotionally involved?*
Imagery, Personal Examples, Rhetorical questions, Created scene

**Logos**
Use of logic or reason. Also pertains to structure of the speech.
Create confidence with statistics and facts.
Creating a sense of reasoning in the audience.

**Types of logical proof**
- Exposition. Proof by definitions and examples
- Argumentation: Reasoning (Inductive and Deductive; Syllogism)
