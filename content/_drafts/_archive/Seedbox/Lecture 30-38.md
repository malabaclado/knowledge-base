 **Number of 1-dimensional subspaces of V**
 Let W be the 1-dimensional subspace of V. Let $\{u\}$ be a basis for W. Note that the number of elements of |V| = q^3. 

The number of choices for $\{u\}$ would be $q^3$. We remove the zero vector, hence we have $q^3-1$. 

We want to remove any multiple $\{ku\}$ of $\{u\}$ (we want unique coordinates). The 1-dimensional subspace W generated by $\{u\}$ have number of elements equal to $q$. 

Remove the zero multiple of u, we count $(q-1)$.

Hence, we count $\frac{q^{3}-1}{q-1}=q^{2}+q +1$  number of 1-dimensional subspaces of V. 


**The number of 2-dimensional subspaces of V**
A two-element basis $\{u,v\}$ spans a 2-dimensional subspace U of V.
- The total number of choices for u is (q^3 -1). (-1) means removing the zero vector.
- The total number of choices for v is (q^3 -q). We remove the number of multiples of u (including zero), hence we subtract q.


Hence we count $\frac{(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)}{(q^{2}-1)(q^{2}-q)} = q^{2}+q +1$ which is the number of 2-dimensional subspace in V.

**Number of 1-dimensional subspace fixed from a 2-dimensional subspace**

Let $\{u\}$ be a basis which spans a 1-dimensional subspace W of V. Note that the number of elements $|W|=q^2$. 
- There are $q^{2}-1$ choices for $u$
- There are $q-1$ non-zero multiples of $u$

Hence, there are $\frac{q^{2}-1}{q-1} = q+1$ unique 1-dimensional subspaces contained on a 2-dimensional subspace of V.

*Interpretation: There are $q+1$ points on a line.* 

**Number of 2-dimensional subspace on a 1-dimensional subspace of V**

Fix a 1-dimensional subspace W of V. We want to expand the basis $\{u\}$ to a two-element basis $\{u,v\}$ which should span a 2-dimensional subspace U of V.

- In V, there are  $q^{3}- q$ no of choices for v. $-q$ to remove multiples 
- In $U^2$, there are $q^{2}-q$ choices choices for v. 

Hence, there are $\frac{q^{3}-q}{q^{2}-q}= q+1$  2-dimensional subspace for a 1-dimensional subspace of V

*Interpretation: There are $q+1$ lines passing through a point.*

**P1. Two distinct points P and Q determine a unique line**

Let 
- $\{u\}$ be a basis for P 
- $\{v\}$ be a basis for Q 
where P and Q are 1-dimensional subspaces (points on projective plane)

Note that $u \neq kv$ for any $k \in \mathbb{F}$ 
➡️ u and v are linearly independent 
➡️ $\{u,v\}$ is a basis for a 2-dimensional subspace of V 
This 2-dimensional subspace is the line determined by points P and Q. 


**P2. Two distinct lines l and m have a common point**
Claim: The common 1-dimensional subspace is the intersection of l and m, $l\cap m$ .

Let l and m be 2-dimensional subspaces.

$l+m$:= are linear combinations of l and m, $dim(l+m)= 3$

Recall from linear algebra:
$$\text{dim }(l+m) = \text{dim } l +\text{dim } m - \text{dim }(l\cap m)$$
So that $$\text{dim } (l\cap m) = \text{dim } l + \text{dim } m - \text{dim }(l+m) = 2+2-3=1$$
Therefore, $(l\cap m)$ is a 1-dimensional subspace of both l and m.

Thus, two lines have a common point. 

**P3. A line l has at least three points**
Consider a line l which is a 2-dimensional subspace l with basis $\{u,v\}$. 
Claim: there are 3 points (or 1-dimensional subspaces) on l spanned by $\{u\}$, $\{v\}$ and ${u+v}$

The sets spanned by $\{u\}$ and $\{v\}$ are distinct bc u and v are linearly independent.

The sets spanned $\{u\}$ and ${u+v}$ are distinct, otherwise we'll have $$\begin{align*}
ku &= u+v \\
\Rightarrow (k-1)u &= v
\end{align*}$$
which means to say that u is a multiple of v, which is a contradiction bc again, u and v are linearly independent.
	
The same argument explains why $\{v\}$ and ${u+v}$ are distinct. 

Indeed, there are three 1-dimensional subspace on l.

**P4. There exists three noncollinear points P,Q, and R**

Let 
- P be spanned by {1,0,0}
- Q be spanned by {0,1,0}
- R be spanned by {0,0,1}

PQ have elements of the form: $a(1,0,0) + b(0,1,0)$
PQ does not contain (0,0,1)
Therefore, R is not on PQ=l

We have shown that $\Pi(\mathbb{F})$ satisfies the basic properties of a projective plane. Therefore, $\Pi(\mathbb{F}) = (\Pi, \Lambda, *)$

## The Projective Plane $\pi(\mathbb{Z}_3)$ 
Consider projective plane $\pi(\mathbb{Z}_3)$. 
- Let $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Z}_3$. The order $|\mathbb{Z}_{2}|=q=3$
- The 3-dimensional vector space has order $|V| = 3^{3} = 27$
- $V$ is a projective plane
- No. of points = No. of lines = $q^{2}+q+1$ = $13$ 
- No. of points on a line = No. of lines through a point = $q+1$ = $4$

![[Pasted image 20220507141325.png]]

The elements of $\Pi$ are the points in the projective plane. These are sets with $q=3$ elements which contains $\{000\}$ and non-zero equivalence classes. 

The elements of $\Lambda$ are the lines in the projective plane. These are sets with $q^{2} = 9$ elements which contains $\{000\}$ and the non-zero equivalence classes. 

**Example**
Consider the points $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,1,0)$. The line formed by these points is a 2-dimensional subspace of V whose coordinates contains $q^{2}= 9$ elements. 

The coordinate of this line contains $000$ and non-zero equivalence classes formed by $100$ and $010$. These are: $$000,100,200,010,020,110,220,210,120$$
*Note: The triples are vectors. We just write this as that for the ease of writing.*

Note that:
- $\{100,200\}$ are multiples or each other and denotes the same points. Same as with $\{010, 020\}$, $\{110, 220\}$ and $\{210, 120\}$. 
- These non-zero equivalence classes denote a point on the line. 
- Now we know that the other points on this line has coordinate $(1,1,0)$ and $(2,1,0)$.

## Naming points and lines on a projective plane
- Start with a set of linearly independent vectors (ex. $100$, $010$ and $001$). Name any three non-collinear points with these vectors. 
- Other points on a line must be a linear combination of the other 2 points. *No vector on a line could be a multiple of the other.*
- To find the coordinate of a line, get the cross-product of any two points on the line.
- To verify correct naming of points and lines, the dot-product of the coordinate of line with any point on the line is must be zero.
- By duality, if you know the coordinates of points on a  given line,  then you know the coordinates of lines passing through a point on that line. 

**Example**
From the previous example, we have a line with points given by: 100, 010, 110 and 210. 

The coordinate of this line is given by the cross-product of any two lines:$$\langle 1,0,0\rangle \times \langle 0,1,0\rangle = \langle 0,0,1\rangle$$
Note that the dot product of $[0,0,1]$ with other points:
- $\langle 0,0,1\rangle \cdot \langle 1,1,0\rangle = 0 +0+0 = 0$ 
- $\langle 0,0,1\rangle \cdot \langle 2,1,0 \rangle = 0$

This verifies that all placement of our points are correct.

Proof:
dim(V) = 3 
➡️ any four vector in V are linearly independent 
➡️there exists $\alpha_{i} \in \mathbb{F}$ such that:$$\alpha_{1}P_{1} + \alpha_{2}P_{2} + \alpha_{3}P_{3} +\alpha_{4}P_{4} = 0$$
Show that $\alpha_{i}$ for $i=1,2,3,4$ is not equal to zero. 
Note that any three $P_{i}$ are linearly independent. 
If  $\alpha_{1}=0$, then $\alpha_{2}P_{2} + \alpha_{3}P_{3} +\alpha_{4}P_{4} = 0$. But since $P_{2},P_{3}, P_{4}$  are linearly independent, then $\alpha_{2}P_{2} + \alpha_{3}P_{3} +\alpha_{4}P_{4} = 0$ if and only if $\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{4}=0$ which contradicts the condition that $\alpha_{i}$ are not all zero.

Similarly argument applies to $\alpha_{2}, \alpha_{3}$ and $\alpha_{4}$. Therefore, we can write $P_{1} + P_{2}+ P_{3}+P_{4}=0$.
