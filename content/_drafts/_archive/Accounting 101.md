# Income Statement
- Income statement reflects  how  an  organization performed as a result of its operations during a specified period.
- This performance report is expressed in financial terms and is based on the fundamental equation: `Revenues - Expenses = Profit/Loss`
- Income statement are also known as *Profit and Loss Statement*
-  **Formats.** Income statements is generally the same in format across organizations but vary  slightly  depending  on  the company ![[Pasted image 20231009161532.png]]
	- ==Service companies do not usually have a cost of goods== because they mainly offer services.
	- Merchandising companies buy"finished goods" and so have a ==separate expense account called *Cost of Goods Sold*==.
	- Manufacturing companies create their own products so they have to note the ==purchase of materials and processes required to make the final product==.
- ## Forms of Income Statement
	- ### Single-step income statement
		- A single step income statement is a financial statement format that ==lists all revenues and expenses in a single column==. It does not break revenues and expenses down into categories and are simply listed together and totaled. ==Smaller and less complicated businesses prefer this format for its simplicity==.

- ## Common income statement accounts
	- Revenue = *total revenue*
		- This is the company's revenue from sales
		- This accounts for the total accumulated value (at selling price) of all services rendered or goods sold using the accrual method (meaning, regardless of whether payment has actually been collected or not) 
	- Cost of goods sold = *total production cost*
		-  This is the total  costs  incurred  by  the  business  in  the  acquisition or manufacture of goods
	- Gross profit = *revenue - cost of goods sold*
	- Operating expenses = *expenses in operating the business*
		- Expenses in operating the business (not due to goods sold). Examples are: rent, employee wages and utilities.
	- Operating income  = *gross profit - operating expenses*
		- Gross profit - Operating Expenses
	- Other revenues & gains/Other expenses & losses
		- These are incidental revenues or expenses that are not in the course of regular business operations but contribute or use up resources of the business.
		- Examples: Interest revenues, rental revenues and gains/losses on sale.
	- Earnings before tax
		- This account is found in income statements of corporations.
		- Corresponds to the corporate income tax mandated by law.
	- Net income
		- The remainder after all expenses are deducted from all revenues.

# Balance Sheet
- Before discussing the balance sheet, it is important to be familiar with different forms of business organizations as ownership structure affects the accounts in the equity components of the Balance Sheet. 
- ## Forms of Business Organizations
	- ### Sole Proprietorship
		- Owned by one person. All transactions are decided by the sole owner.
		- Minimal regulation in terms of registration and taxation.
		- Income generated from business is treated as owner's personal income for tax and other regulatory purposes.
	- ### Partnership
		- Owned by more than one person. There are two major types.
		- Partnerships allow for more access to resources as there are more owners than a sole proprietorship. 
		- #### General partnerships
			- composed of partners who are all personally and solidarily liable for the business entity’s debts and obligations.  
		- #### Limited partnerships
			-  consists of at least one general partner and any number of limited partners.
	- ### Corporation
		- A corporation is *“an artificial being created by operation of law.”* 
		- Because it is its own ‘person’, liabilities and obligations of owners are limited to their investment in the business.
		- As a legal entity, corporations are also mandated to pay its own income taxes. 
- ## What is a balance sheet?
	- The Balance Sheet ==reports the financial position of an entity at a given date==. 
	- Unlike the income statement that presents temporary accounts within a specific period, the balance sheet presents the accumulated balances of permanent accounts.
	- It consists of a list of all assets, liabilities and equity (net worth) of an entity.
	- The  balance  sheet  may  be  presented  in  report  form  (single  column  for  all accounts) or in account form (one column for assets, another for liabilities and equity). Some balance sheets are also presented in a classified format, where assets and liabilities are further segregated in to current and non-current components.

- ## The Operating Cycle
	- A discussion of the classified balance sheet entails understanding of an entity’s operating  cycle  for  the  determination  of  current  and  non-current  accounts. 
	- **The operating  cycle**  is  the  average  period  required  for  a  business  to  acquire goods/services, make the sale, and collect payment on said sales. 
	- The general rule for classifying an asset as current is if it is expected to be used or sold within the operating cycle or one year  from  reporting  date,  whichever  is  longer.
	- In  the  case  of  liabilities,  these  are considered current if they are expected to be paid within the same period as the current assets. All other assets and liabilities are considered non-current. 
- ## Balance Sheet Accounts
	- ### Assets
		- Cash and Cash equivalents
			- Cash  is  legal  tender.
			-  Cash equivalents are highly liquid, very low risk financial products with original maturity periods of three months or less. Treasury bills and time deposits are some examples. 
		- Accounts receivable
			- Payments made through credit transactions (which are recieved at a later date but needs to be accounted for)
		- Inventory
			- Merchandising  and  manufacturing  entities  must  record  the  historical cost of goods that they have for sale. 
		- Prepaid expenses
			- Payments made in advance for use of services.
			- Because they offer a future benefit, they are recorded as assets until such time that they are expensed/used.
		- Investments
			- Businesses  may  sometimes  have  an  excess  amount  of  cash  that management decides to put in securities in order to optimize income. 
	- ### Liabilities
		- Accounts payable
			- Amount supplied to suppliers who extend credit. Reverse of *accounts receivable*.
		- Unearned revenues
			- Sales/services paid for in advance by customers are recorde agains this liability until such time that the obligations are fulfilled.
		- Other payables
			- Includes wages, rent, utilities
		- Long term debts
			- Often  arising  from  borrowing  activities  done  to  augment  sources  of funds, this could be notes payable, mortgage payable, bonds payable, etc.
	- ### Equity
		- Owner/Partner's Capital
			- These are the contributions made into the business by its owners 
		- Capital stocks
			- These are shares in the business acquired by investors (hence owners) of a corporation.
		- Additional paid-in capital
			- When  stocks  of  a  business  are  acquired  by  investors  by  paying  a consideration worth  more  than  the  par  value,  the  excess  amount  is recorded as additional paid-in capital or paid-in capital in excess of par. 
		- Retained earnings
			- Businesses  formed  as  corporations,  being  juridically  separate  from investors, possess the right to all profits earned from its operations. Only when distribution of income in the form of dividends is approved by the board of directors can these be paid out to the investors, unlike in sole proprietorships or partnerships where owners may withdraw at any time. Since the legal capital may not be distributed to investors for any  reason  other  than  dissolution,  profits  or  losses  throughout  the corporation’s existence are accumulated into a separate account called retained earnings. This is the only account where dividends may be charged against. 

# Common financial ratios
- ## Debt ratio
	- Total liabilities/Total Assets
- ## Current ratio
	- Current assets/Current liabilities
- ## Working capital
	- Current assets - current liabilities
- ## Assets-to-Equity ratio
	- Total assets/Owner's equity
- ## Debt-to-Equity Ratio
	- Total liabilities/Owner's equity

# Common financial ratio (from chatgpt)

1. **Profit Margin**: Profit margin measures a company's profitability by comparing its net income to its revenue. It is expressed as a percentage. A higher profit margin indicates that a company is more efficient in converting its sales into profit.
2. **Return on Assets (ROA)**: ROA assesses a company's ability to generate profits from its assets. It is calculated by dividing net income by average total assets. A higher ROA signifies better asset utilization and efficiency.
3. **Return on Equity (ROE)**: ROE measures how effectively a company uses shareholders' equity to generate profits. It is calculated by dividing net income by shareholders' equity. A higher ROE indicates stronger financial performance.
4. **Earnings Per Share (EPS)**: EPS represents the portion of a company's profit allocated to each outstanding share of common stock. It's calculated by dividing net income by the number of outstanding shares. Higher EPS is generally seen as a positive sign for shareholders.
5. **Price-to-Earnings Ratio (P/E)**: The P/E ratio compares a company's stock price to its earnings per share. It reflects the market's expectations for future earnings growth. A higher P/E ratio can indicate a higher growth expectation but may also suggest an overvalued stock.
6. **Debt-to-Equity Ratio**: This ratio measures a company's leverage by comparing its total debt to shareholders' equity. A higher debt-to-equity ratio indicates a higher level of financial risk and leverage.
7. **Current Ratio**: The current ratio assesses a company's short-term liquidity by dividing current assets by current liabilities. A ratio greater than 1 indicates that the company can cover its short-term obligations.
8. **Quick Ratio (Acid-Test Ratio)**: The quick ratio is a more stringent measure of liquidity, excluding inventory from current assets. It's calculated by dividing quick assets (current assets minus inventory) by current liabilities. A ratio above 1 suggests better short-term liquidity.
9. **Gross Margin**: Gross margin measures a company's profitability before considering operating expenses. It is calculated by subtracting the cost of goods sold (COGS) from revenue and dividing the result by revenue. A higher gross margin indicates better pricing and cost control.
10. **Dividend Yield**: Dividend yield is the annual dividend payment per share divided by the stock's current market price. It helps investors assess the income they can earn from owning a particular stock.
11. **Asset Turnover Ratio**: This ratio evaluates a company's efficiency in using its assets to generate sales. It is calculated by dividing revenue by average total assets. A higher asset turnover ratio suggests effective asset utilization.
12. **Inventory Turnover Ratio**: Inventory turnover measures how quickly a company sells its inventory. It is calculated by dividing the cost of goods sold (COGS) by the average inventory value. A higher ratio implies efficient inventory management.
13. **Operating Margin**: Operating margin measures a company's profitability from its core operations by dividing operating income by revenue. It indicates how efficiently a company manages its operating expenses.
14. **Debt Ratio**: The debt ratio compares a company's total debt to its total assets. It provides a broader perspective on a company's financial leverage and solvency.
15. **Interest Coverage Ratio**: This ratio assesses a company's ability to cover its interest expenses with its earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT). A higher ratio indicates a company's capacity to meet its interest obligations.
16. **Price-to-Sales Ratio (P/S)**: The P/S ratio compares a company's stock price to its revenue per share. It's a valuation metric that doesn't consider profitability and is often used for companies with low or negative earnings.
17. **Cash Ratio**: The cash ratio measures a company's ability to cover its short-term liabilities using its cash and cash equivalents alone. It's a stringent liquidity measure.
18. **Return on Investment (ROI)**: ROI evaluates the return generated from a particular investment or project. It's calculated by dividing the net gain from the investment by the initial cost of the investment.
19. **Inventory Turnover in Days**: This ratio measures how many days, on average, it takes for a company to sell its inventory. It can help assess the efficiency of inventory management and cash flow.
20. **Accounts Receivable Turnover**: Accounts receivable turnover assesses how quickly a company collects payments from its customers. It is calculated by dividing annual credit sales by the average accounts receivable balance.
21. **Price-to-Book Ratio (P/B)**: The P/B ratio compares a company's stock price to its book value per share (total assets minus total liabilities divided by the number of outstanding shares). It indicates whether a stock is undervalued or overvalued.
22. **Dividend Payout Ratio**: The dividend payout ratio measures the proportion of earnings a company pays out as dividends to shareholders. It's calculated by dividing dividends per share by earnings per share.
23. **Working Capital Turnover**: This ratio evaluates how effectively a company uses its working capital to generate sales. It's calculated by dividing revenue by working capital (current assets minus current liabilities).
24. **Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization (EBITDA) Margin**: EBITDA margin assesses a company's profitability by looking at earnings before certain non-cash expenses and financing costs. It's calculated by dividing EBITDA by revenue.
25. **Quick Assets to Current Liabilities**: This ratio, similar to the quick ratio, measures a company's ability to meet short-term obligations, but it includes only the most liquid assets (cash, marketable securities, and accounts receivable) divided by current liabilities.
# Cash Flow Statement
- The cash flow statement is a report that ==shows how well a company manages its cash position==. It explains the change in ==cash balance from the beginning to the end of a period== and shows how cash has been moved around during the period.
- Example of a cash flow statement: https://www.vertex42.com/ExcelTemplates/cash-flow-statement.html
- ## Main components of a Cash Flow Statement
	- ### Operating activities
		- Include sources and uses of cash from business activities. These are the transactions that enter into the determination of net income. 
		- There are two methods of preparing this component:
			- #### Direct Method
				- Systematically  going  down  the  list  of  income statement items and calculating how much cash is associated with each item. Example: [Intro to Cash Flow Statements | Direct Method - YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xy-yDw0gsgc)
			- #### Indirect Method
				- Begins from the last item in the income statement, the net income. From this figure, adjustments are made to remove non-cash transactions and properly reflect cash flow numbers. Example: [Prepare A Cash Flow Statement | Indirect Method - YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8CH-6wdfz0Y)
	- ### Investing activities
		- Acquisition and disposal of non-current fixed assets. ==These pieces of information are derived from the non-current asset portion of the balance sheet.==
	- ### Financing activities
		- Includes transacions that involve cash receipt or payment to owners and creditors. ==This incofmation comes from the non-current liabilities and the equity portion of the balance sheet.==
